I was holding him, crying, and one thought kept looping: *I have to keep this little person alive.*

Nine months earlier, we had planned everything.

The pregnancy itself was planned. Very planned. Nehrini (my wife, who writes [The Motherhood Manual](https://nehrini.github.io/the-motherhood-manual/)) and I had our life settled: a house, our cars, stable jobs. We felt well placed to start this adventure. And we were lucky: it happened faster than we expected.

I found out in the middle of the night. Nehrini told me she needed to take a home test. I went to look, the result came up, and she said: "Well, it's positive." I got incredibly emotional on the spot. It was what we wanted, and we'd done it.

## Getting ready

A few days later it hit me: we had to get ready.

We live in Ireland. Our parents and the rest of our family are far away, in other countries. We have friends, but naturally you can't ask friends to come over, cook for you, do your chores and look after the baby. So we had to figure out how to replace the family help most people get at this point.

We saw two options. We could hire nurses or midwives to come to the house and look after the baby for a few hours. Or we could automate our chores and buy machines to take over the work we'd otherwise do ourselves.

We both work in IT, so naturally we went with the second one: let the machines do the job, and we'll spend the time with the baby.

A dishwasher for the bottles and dummies. A machine to warm the milk. A tumble dryer, because we didn't have one. A small freezer, just for the meals we cooked ahead, because we knew we wouldn't have time to cook. And plenty more gadgets like that.

Looking back today, it was a good decision: they helped us enormously day to day and took a lot of pressure off.

A few of my colleagues had recently had babies, so naturally I started asking them for tips. Give me the good things, the bad things, the wonderful things and the horrible things. I took notes on all of it.

And we watched every course and every video about pregnancy, about caring for a baby, about the first month. We never lacked information. It was scattered, but it was never missing. It was overwhelming, though: far more than you actually need, and a lot of it written for realities that weren't ours, for countries where people live differently, with different laws and different ways of doing things. We did all of it anyway.

…and I thought I was ready.
{: .highlight}

## Then he arrived

The day came and my son was born. I cried.

And the moment I held him, I realised I really wasn't. I'm responsible now. Responsible for keeping a living being alive.

It's such an overwhelming moment. You feel happy holding him. But you also feel the enormous, enormous responsibility of caring for someone who can't talk, who can't tell you what he feels, and whose only way of telling you anything is to cry. And you have to understand that, accept that new reality, and take on that new job.

No checklist, no gadget and no freezer full of food prepares you for that part.
{: .highlight}

If you're about to become a dad and you think you're ready: you're not. Nobody is. And that's okay.
